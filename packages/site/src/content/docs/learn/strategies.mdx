ireko has three strategies: `grid`, `radial` and `tree`. A strategy arranges the children of one node. It gets the size of each child and the extent of its subtree, and it gives an offset for each child. The engine does all other work: the recursion, the padding, the fit and the absolute positions. Thus a strategy is a small function, and you can [write your own](/learn/custom-strategy/).

## Try each option

The controls come from the option schema of each strategy. The engine uses the same schema to examine the options of a node.

## grid

The grid strategy puts the children in rows and columns. Each column is as wide as its widest child, and each row is as tall as its tallest child. A child counts with its whole subtree, so a large subtree gets a large cell.

<Figure source={tracks} caption="A linked grid: the column of the subtree large is wide. The other columns stay narrow." height="14rem" />

The number of columns depends on the mode:

- In the `linked` mode, the default is one row. Then no edge crosses a sibling box. With `side: left` or `side: right`, the default is one column.
- In the `nested` mode, the default is an approximately square grid.
- Set `columns` to select a number.

In the linked mode, `side` selects where the children go:

## radial

The radial strategy puts the children on a ring. Each child subtree fits in a circle around the center of its box. Two neighbors need an angle that keeps their circles apart by the gap. On a full ring, the strategy finds the smallest radius at which these angles fill the ring. Thus a small child takes less of the ring than a large child.

<Figure source={rings} caption="A ring with children of different sizes. The large child gets a large part of the ring." height="20rem" />

With `sweep` less than 360, the children go on an arc. Each child is at the middle of an equal part of the arc, and the largest circle sets the radius.

## tree

The tree strategy puts the children in one row, on one side of the node, like the linked grid. The grid keeps the extents of the subtrees apart: the rectangles that hold each subtree. The tree strategy keeps their shapes apart. Thus a subtree that is narrow at the top fits under the edge of its neighbor. This layout is a tidy tree.

<Morph client:visible title="An organization chart: grid or tree" height="20rem" states={tidyStates} />

The strategy packs the row two times: from the first child to the last, and from the last to the first. Then it takes the mean of the two places of each child. Both packings keep each pair of subtrees apart, so the mean keeps them apart too. The small subtrees go to the middle of their space.

The tree strategy gives the direction of its edges to the router. With the `orthogonal` router, the edges make the buses of an organization chart. The [gallery](/gallery/tidy-tree/) has this chart with labels.

## Options of each container

Each strategy also has `gap`, `padding` and `header`. The engine reads them, not the strategy. [Modes and fit](/learn/modes/#gap-padding-and-header) explains them.

## Write the options

The text language puts the options in parentheses after the name of the strategy. The builder takes them in an object. Plain objects use `layout: { type, ...options }`.

```text
team grid(columns: 3, uniform: true) nested { a b c d e }
```

```ts
grid.nested("team", { columns: 3, uniform: true }, "a", "b", "c", "d", "e");

const same = { id: "team", mode: "nested", layout: { type: "grid", columns: 3, uniform: true }, children: [/* ... */] };
```

An option with a wrong type or out of its range gives the finding [`INVALID_OPTION`](/reference/issues/#invalid_option), and the strategy uses the default.
